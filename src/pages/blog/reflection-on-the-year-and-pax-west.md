Fog rolled over the horizon of Elliot Bay. I sipped on a hot latte and gazed out at the water at Pike Place Market, and I felt the tension begin to lift from my shoulders. It wasn’t gone, not completely, though it would vanish entirely several days later. No, this was the first morning of PAX West and I had spent a year prior to this day agonizing over a vast monolith of stress: questioning a change of career in the oncoming "Agentic Revolution" and feeling that I no longer have a place in the field that I have always loved, coping with the encroaching rise of fascism from within our nation, wrestling to develop a better understanding my relationship with gender and my still-developing non-binary identity, the death of a mentor and dear friend to suicide and asking the question "could I have been a better friend", a six-month long illness that shattered my lungs like glass, and reeling from the experience of witnessing family turn on family. No, this was too soon. I finished my coffee, picked up my bag, and made the last leg of my trek uphill to the Seattle Convention Center Summit Building.

I was greeted on arrival by the sounds of a lone musician, proudly playing his violin to the opening theme of Pokemon Red and Blue. It fit, marking the beginning of a bold new adventure as the early attendees made their way across the stone courtyard to the opening queues. I couldn’t shake the feeling, however, that this was the end of my own adventure. I strode through the metal detectors with my bright red backpack and I reminisced over the last decade of tech and digital media. I wondered if art and human connection could flourish within tech in the days ahead of us, if that world could harbor a place for those of us resistant to AI zealotry, and if I had outgrown my love of digital mediums all together. I made peace that this event could have been my final departure from the field I’ve loved since I was fourteen years old.

For two consecutive days each and every speaker chipped away at my obtuse, overt, and self-defeatist, stupidity. From a full ballroom of fans celebrating Persona 4 and cheering on the nervous cosplayers that Atlus invited on stage, to Valorant’s art director and level designer breaking down the cultural significance of the newly released Summit map, to celebrity DM Kate Welch directing a full auditorium of players as a mass, and very silly, collaborative D&D party, each panel brought to the forefront a joy and care that rests at the nexus of our craft. Accessibility expert and programmer, Sara Winters, displayed mastery in her abilities as a [legally blind](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C6xz58O4xq8) FFXIV savage raider, while using the opportunity to educate the masses on the importance of accessible game design for the disabled. Lastly, to cap off the second night, under bright green lights and the against the backdrop of thick billowing fog, I got to watch my prime inspiration as a Dungeon Master, Christopher Perkins, lead a session of the game he now dedicates his career to build.

I broke on day three, though the moment wouldn’t happen until long after leaving the convention center. Celebration of games is only one aspect of events like this. The people we spend time with, I would argue, are what matter the most. I am, for lack of better words, a profoundly lonely person ( It is very possible to feel lonely in a crowd ). And it is in the depths of self-isolation where I think that I’ve learned to value human connection the most. That afternoon, I got to mark an item long-standing on my bucket list: playing D&D and Magic, in-person, with two of my closest friends. It had been almost a decade since we had last seen each other outside of Discord. I also got to meet up with another good friend of mine, a deeply respected colleague and former classmate from UCF, who graciously led me on a tour of Capital Hill and introduced me to new friends, before we finally met up with her spouse for ramen. It was that hour in between PAX and dinner, as the sun started to set and we wandered Cal Anderson Park, that I decided to take pictures of the ducks. Just ducks. Simple, harmless, ducks on a swim in the warm light of the setting sun and the crystal clear waters of the fountain’s pool. Most of the pictures were terrible. I didn’t care.

*"It will," Wit said, "but then it will get better. Then it will get worse again. Then better. This is life, and I will not lie by saying every day will be sunshine. But there will be sunshine again, and that is a very different thing to say. That is truth. I promise you, Kaladin: You will be warm again." – Brandon Sanderson, Rhythem of War*

I had been chasing that feeling for so very long -- and losing hope. But, I was, at long last, warm again.

I wrote this the evening before my final day of PAX, but far from the end of my stay in Seattle. For, we should not relish in the closing of chapters, but rather celebrate new beginnings, new friends, and new games to build and play. As Wit said, not every day will be sunshine. But, it is games and stories that have the power to bring us together as people. I had always believed that before, and I guess I still do. I only needed to let others into my life to help me rediscover that joy. The final day of PAX may be a disaster. It could also be life changing in ways that I could never predict. It could also just be another day and that’s OK.

**Reporting in from the tail end of my trip, now several days later and with sore blistered feet and a wicked sunburn:** I had spoken with nothing short of half a dozen locals outide of PAX, across multiple neighborhoods and all walks of life, who also attended the event or wished that they could have. One person, a front desk worker in my hotel’s lobby, worked a booth the entire four days and recognized me from my oversized camera and PAX hoodie. We connected for a brief moment, never sharing names, but both of us were alight in joy over our shared love of games.

For all of the cynicism, darkness, and uncertainty in the world of tech in 2026, games retain the unwavering power to connect us all, forge memories, and bring between us a joy that transcends all.
